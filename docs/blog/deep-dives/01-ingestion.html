Why this post

The eval plan follows one question through 12 steps. Each step got a few paragraphs there. This series gives each step its own post with the real code, so a reader can see exactly how it works, not just what it does.

Ingestion (preparing documents ahead of time: cut into chunks, tagged and stored for search) comes first because every later step depends on it. Search can only find what ingestion (preparing documents ahead of time: cut into chunks, tagged and stored for search) stored, and the AI can only quote what search found. If a rule is cut in half here, no later step can put it back together.

How this differs from cutting by characters. The usual way is to count characters: cut every 500, wherever that lands. It is fast and works on any text, but it doesn't know what a rule is. It cuts mid-rule and even mid-word, and it glues the end of one rule to the start of the next. NurseOps Now turns this around: the policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered)'s structure decides where to cut, and characters only set a size limit.

How we break it up, in order of priority:

  1. Structure first: sections and rules. Cut at every numbered section ("3. Required documentation") and every lettered rule ("A. Suspected meniscus (the cartilage cushion inside the knee)…"). The policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered)'s author already decided where one rule ends, so we use their boundaries. One chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) is one complete rule.
  2. Context carried down. Each lettered rule keeps the title of its section ("Covered when ONE of the following is documented") and gets a header with the policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered), version, CPT code (a standard code for a medical procedure) and page. A small rule still says whose rule it is and what it means when read alone.
  3. Characters second, only as a safety net. Only if one rule is longer than 1,200 characters is it split again. Even then it tries natural breaks first (paragraph, then line, then sentence), repeats the last 150 characters at the start of the next piece, and only cuts at a raw character count if there is no break at all.

Why characters are the backup and not the main rule:

  • The limit comes from the model, not the policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered). The embedding (text turned into numbers so similar meanings sit close together) model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (a small, free open model that turns text into 384 numbers)) reads at most 256 word-pieces (the small word parts a model reads; a long word may be several), about 1,000–1,200 characters, and silently ignores the rest (a common way for programs to talk over the web using URLs). A rule longer than that would be only partly embedded (turned into numbers (a vector) so similar meanings sit close together), and its end could never be found. So characters cap the size, but they never pick the boundary.
  • Splitting at a break beats splitting at a count. When a split is needed, a paragraph or sentence break keeps whole thoughts together, and the overlap (characters repeated between neighboring chunks so nothing is lost at a cut) means a sentence crossing the cut appears whole in at least one piece.
  • Today the safety net never fires. The longest of the 83 chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) is 487 characters, so every chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) in the index (the searchable store of chunks and their vectors) is exactly one rule. The net is there for a future policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered) with a very long rule.

What we added to this idea:

  • There is no minimum size. Character-based chunking usually also merges small pieces to reach a target size. We don't: the shortest rule (§2.D Planning for knee surgery, 132 characters with its header) stays its own chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule). Merging it into its neighbor would mix two rules, and the AI could cite the wrong one. The header gives a short chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) the context its body lacks.
  • No overlap (characters repeated between neighboring chunks so nothing is lost at a cut) between rules. Character chunking repeats text between neighboring pieces (often 10–20%) to patch cuts that land mid-sentence. Our cuts land where a rule ends, so there is nothing to patch, and the chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) don't overlap (characters repeated between neighboring chunks so nothing is lost at a cut) at all. Adding overlap (characters repeated between neighboring chunks so nothing is lost at a cut) here would do harm: the end of §2.A would appear inside §2.B's chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule), so a search could return §2.B for a question about §2.A, and the AI could cite the wrong section. The only overlap (characters repeated between neighboring chunks so nothing is lost at a cut) is the 150 characters between pieces of one long rule when the fallback (the backup method used only when the main one can't work) splits it, and it never crosses into another rule.
  • The cut decides what can be cited. The AI must quote a rule and give its § and page, and a later step matches every quote against the PDF (a fixed-layout document file). With character cuts, a chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) could start in §2.A and end in §2.B, so no single § or page would be true for it. With structure cuts, every chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) has exactly one § and one page.

In plain terms: before a librarian can answer questions, someone has to put the books on the shelves, with labels. This post is about that someone.

Sketch: pull, hash and chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) KB articles (one help-center article in a knowledge base, with a number like KB0010234) (Python standard library, plus requests for the API call; saved as tools/scripts/kb_chunk_sketch.py)

import hashlib
from html.parser import HTMLParser

FIELDS = "sys_id,number,short_description,text,kb_knowledge_base,kb_category,sys_updated_on,can_read_user_criteria"
SKIP_CLASSES = {"kb-banner", "kb-feedback"}      # template boilerplate, not content
SKIP_SECTIONS = {"Related articles"}             # links only: no answer in them


def fetch_published(instance: str, auth, kb_sys_id: str):
    """Page through published articles in one knowledge base (REST Table API)."""
    import requests
    url = f"https://{instance}.serviceapps.com/api/now/table/kb_knowledge"
    offset = 0
    while True:
        r = requests.get(url, auth=auth, timeout=30, headers={"Accept": "application/json"}, params={
            "sysparm_query": f"workflow_state=published^kb_knowledge_base={kb_sys_id}",
            "sysparm_fields": FIELDS, "sysparm_display_value": "all",   # value + display_value per field
            "sysparm_limit": 100, "sysparm_offset": offset})
        r.raise_for_status()
        rows = r.json()["result"]
        if not rows:
            return
        yield from rows
        offset += len(rows)


def content_hash(a) -> str:
    """Fingerprint of the article body: re-embed only when this changes."""
    return hashlib.sha256(a["text"]["value"].encode()).hexdigest()


class Blocks(HTMLParser):
    """Flatten article HTML into blocks: ("heading", t), ("line", t), ("row", cells, is_header), ("skip", t)."""

    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.blocks, self.text, self.cells, self.skip, self.lists = [], [], [], None, []

    def handle_starttag(self, tag, attrs):
        if self.skip is None and dict(attrs).get("class") in SKIP_CLASSES:
            self.skip = tag
        if tag in ("ol", "ul"):
            self.lists.append([tag, 0])
        if tag == "tr":
            self.cells, self.header_row = [], False
        if tag == "th":
            self.header_row = True
        if tag in ("h2", "h3", "p", "li", "td", "th") or tag == self.skip:
            self.text = []

    def handle_data(self, data):
        self.text.append(data)

    def handle_endtag(self, tag):
        t = " ".join("".join(self.text).split())
        if tag == self.skip:
            self.blocks.append(("skip", t))
            self.skip = None
        elif self.skip:
            return
        elif tag in ("ol", "ul"):
            self.lists.pop()
        elif tag in ("td", "th"):
            self.cells.append(t)
        elif tag == "tr":
            self.blocks.append(("row", self.cells, self.header_row))
        elif tag in ("h2", "h3"):
            self.blocks.append(("heading", t))
        elif tag == "li":
            kind = self.lists[-1] if self.lists else ["ul", 0]
            kind[1] += 1
            self.blocks.append(("line", f"{kind[1]}. {t}" if kind[0] == "ol" else f"- {t}"))
        elif tag == "p":
            self.blocks.append(("line", t))


def units(html: str) -> list[tuple[str, list[str]]]:
    """(heading, lines) per chunk: one per heading section, one per table row; numbered steps stay together."""
    p = Blocks()
    p.feed(html)
    out, heading = [], "Overview"
    for b in p.blocks:
        if b[0] == "heading":
            heading = b[1]
            out.append((heading, []))
        elif heading in SKIP_SECTIONS or b[0] == "skip":
            continue
        elif b[0] == "line":
            if not out:
                out.append((heading, []))
            out[-1][1].append(b[1])
        elif b[0] == "row" and not b[2]:                   # one chunk per row, named by its first cell
            out.append((b[1][0], [" ".join(b[1][1:])]))
    return [(h, lines) for h, lines in out if lines]       # a heading with no text of its own isn't a chunk


def chunk_article(a) -> list[dict]:
    """One chunk per section or table row, each with a header naming the article: the KB version of 'one per clause'."""
    num, title = a["number"]["value"], a["short_description"]["value"]
    kb, cat = a["kb_knowledge_base"]["display_value"], a["kb_category"]["display_value"]
    return [{"id": f"{num}-{n}",
             "text": f"[{num} | {kb} | {cat} | {title} | {h}]\n" + "\n".join(lines),
             "meta": {"sys_id": a["sys_id"]["value"], "number": num, "kb": kb, "category": cat,
                      "section": h, "updated": a["sys_updated_on"]["value"],
                      "can_read": a["can_read_user_criteria"]["value"]}}
            for n, (h, lines) in enumerate(units(a["text"]["value"]))]

Alternative docs: when articles don't follow a template

Everything above assumes articles follow a template with real headings, steps and tables. Many knowledge bases (a library of help articles; in ServiceApps, a named group of KB articles) also hold articles that don't: old migrated articles, one long block of text, pasted email threads, attached Word or PDF (a fixed-layout document file) files. For those, cutting at headings doesn't work, because there are no headings to cut at. Decide per article:

A cut might fall between "Restart the client" and "…then clear the cache". The overlap (characters repeated between neighboring chunks so nothing is lost at a cut) means that pair appears whole in at least one piece, so search can still find it.

Add-ons that work on top of any of them:

  • Contextual headers (the label at the top of each chunk: policy, version, CPT code, section and page): the [KB number | knowledge base | category | title | section] line above, or a one-line summary of the whole article added to each chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) (often called contextual retrieval (adding a short summary of the whole document to each chunk before embedding)). Helps every splitter.
  • Parent–child (search small chunks, then give the AI the bigger section they came from) ("small-to-big"): search small chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) for precision, then give the AI the larger section they came from.
  • Sentence window (store single sentences and return each with its neighbors): store single sentences, and return each one with its neighbors.
  • Late chunking (embedding a whole document first, then splitting the result into chunks): embed the whole article with a long-context model first, then split, so each chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) "knows" the rest (a common way for programs to talk over the web using URLs). Needs special models.

Usually not worth it as the main method: AI-rewritten "proposition (a single standalone fact rewritten by an AI from the text)" chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) (high quality but slow and expensive at scale), and fixed-size (cutting text every N characters, wherever that lands) cuts with no overlap (characters repeated between neighboring chunks so nothing is lost at a cut) (useful only as a baseline (the simple version you compare improvements against) to compare against).

How to choose:

  1. Template with headings → structure first.
  2. Visual headings in attached files → layout-aware parsing (reading a file's layout (bold headings, lists, tables) to find its structure).
  3. No structure at all → recursive splitter (code that splits text by paragraph, then line, then sentence, until each piece fits) with overlap (characters repeated between neighboring chunks so nothing is lost at a cut); try semantic chunking (cutting text where the meaning changes) only if evals (graded tests of the AI's answers, scored against an answer key) show recursive missing answers.

The job. The problem. A policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered) is a 5-page PDF (a fixed-layout document file). Sub-step 5: Split long clauses

What it does. If one clause (one rule in a policy, such as §2.A) is longer than 1,200 characters, it is split again: first at blank lines, then at line breaks, then at sentence ends, with 150 characters repeated between pieces. Only as a last resort is it cut every 1,050 characters.

The code.

MAX_CHARS = 1200      # recursive fallback above this
OVERLAP = 150

def _split_long(text: str) -> list[str]:
    """Recursive fallback: paragraphs -> lines -> hard cut, with overlap."""
    if len(text) <= MAX_CHARS:
        return [text]
    for sep in ("\n\n", "\n", ". "):
        parts = text.split(sep)
        if len(parts) > 1:
            out, cur = [], ""
            for p in parts:
                cand = f"{cur}{sep}{p}" if cur else p
                if len(cand) > MAX_CHARS and cur:
                    out.append(cur)
                    cur = cur[-OVERLAP:] + sep + p      # carry the last 150 chars forward
                else:
                    cur = cand
            out.append(cur)
            return [s for piece in out for s in _split_long(piece)] if any(len(o) > MAX_CHARS for o in out) else out
    return [text[i:i + MAX_CHARS] for i in range(0, len(text), MAX_CHARS - OVERLAP)]

The right rule scores higher, but only by 0.075. That narrow gap is why the later steps exist: keyword search (search for the exact words in the question), query rewriting (restating a question in the policy's own wording before searching) and re-ranking (a second model reorders the top results) (Steps 4–6) push the right rule clearly to the top.

Why this way.

  • Local and free: no API cost per chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule), no policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered) text sent out to embed, and it runs offline.
  • Normalized (scaled to length 1, so similarity is a simple multiply-and-add) vectors (text turned into lists of numbers so similar meanings sit close together) (normalize_embeddings=True), so cosine similarity (a score from -1 to 1 for how close two meanings are) is a plain dot product (multiply two lists of numbers pair by pair and add the results) and Chroma (the database that stores the policy vectors)'s cosine distance matches it.
  • Small and fast: 384 numbers per chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule), 83 chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) in total. A larger model would cost more memory on the Fly.io (the cloud service that hosts the live app) machine for little gain at this size. Part C of the eval plan is where a model comparison would go.
  • Tests never download it. They swap in HashEmbedder, a tiny word-counting embedder, through set_embedder(), so the 11 RAG (retrieval-augmented generation: the AI looks things up before it answers) tests run offline in about a second.

In plain terms: giving every card a map coordinate so cards about the same thing sit close together.

def index_policy(p: dict) -> dict:
    path, _ = policy_svc.policy_path(p["cpt"])
    chunks = chunk_policy(path, p)
    flagged = [c.id for c in chunks if c.meta["flagged"]]
    keep = [c for c in chunks if not c.meta["flagged"]]

Honest gaps

  • The clause (one rule in a policy, such as §2.A) choice isn't measured yet. The fixed-size (cutting text every N characters, wherever that lands) example above shows a rule cut in half, but there is no score yet saying how much clause (one rule in a policy, such as §2.A) chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) improve search. Part C of the eval plan runs the same questions with fixed-size (cutting text every N characters, wherever that lands) chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) and with clause (one rule in a policy, such as §2.A) chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) and reports hit@1 (the right section came back first) and MRR (a score for how high the right section ranks: 1st = 1, 2nd = 0.5, 3rd = 0.33) for both.
  • There is no content hash (a fingerprint of a file that changes whenever the file changes) today. The feature blog says "a content hash (a fingerprint of a file that changes whenever the file changes) re-indexes (rebuild the searchable store after documents change) only the files that changed". The code doesn't do that: index-policies re-reads and re-embeds every policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered) (83 chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each), a few seconds), and a search rebuilds the index (the searchable store of chunks and their vectors) only when it is empty. Each policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered) is replaced as a whole, so there are no stale duplicates, but nothing detects which file changed. Either the code needs a hash per file, or the feature blog needs correcting.
  • Only the newest version is searchable. Re-indexing deletes every older version of a policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered). Reviews store the chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) and version they used, so past decisions stay auditable, but a case can't be re-run against last year's version.
  • One known retrieval (finding the right section of the right policy) miss starts here. In Step 0, "What documents are required for a knee MRI (a scan that uses magnets to show soft tissue like discs, ligaments and the brain)?" returned §17 Coding notes instead of §3 Required documentation. Both chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) are short and both mention requests and documents. Whether the fix belongs in ingestion (preparing documents ahead of time: cut into chunks, tagged and stored for search) (for example, adding the section title to the keyword index (the searchable store of chunks and their vectors) more strongly) or in search is a question for Part C.

What this proves

Ingestion (preparing documents ahead of time: cut into chunks, tagged and stored for search) in NurseOps Now is a deliberate design, not a library default.
