Why this post

What this covers. Context and state (everything the app must remember to continue correctly) management in four parts: managing the window (7.1), compressing history (7.2), saving state (everything the app must remember to continue correctly) outside the model (7.3), and keeping a long-running agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) on task and truthful (7.4), after an intro on the core problem. Every problem and solution is also tagged with the memory type it deals with: working, episodic, semantic or procedural (the four memory types).

How this differs from the other posts. The ingestion deep dive is about finding the right policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered) text (RAG (retrieval-augmented generation: the AI looks things up before it answers)). The eval plan is about proving answers are good. This post is about memory: what the AI is given on each call, what is saved, for whom, for how long, and what must never be summarized or leaked. Each step has the two usual halves: the strategy (what we built) and the proof (how it's checked).

Context and State Management (Intro)

The problem. A language model is stateless (keeping no memory between calls): it remembers nothing between calls. Whatever the AI should know (the rules, the conversation so far, the case facts) has to be sent again on every call, inside the context window (everything sent to the AI in one call; it has a size limit). That window is limited in size, paid for by the token (the unit the AI reads and is billed by, about three quarters of a word), and gone as soon as the call ends. An app can fail in two opposite ways:

  • Too much in the window. The call gets slow and expensive, and the rules sent at the start get less attention, so the AI can lose its instructions.
  • Nothing saved outside it. After a restart, a crash or a new session, the AI starts from zero.

Implementing Short-Term Memory: Token Budgets and Window Management (7.1)

What it does. Keeps each call's context window (everything sent to the AI in one call; it has a size limit) small and on budget: count tokens (pieces of words the AI reads and writes; cost is per token), load facts only when needed, and pin the rules so they're never pushed out.

The problem:

  1. [Working] The token window is a hard limit (everything sent to the AI shares one fixed size budget): the rules, the question and the whole history share one fixed budget.
  2. [Working · Episodic] Sliding window truncation (silently dropping the oldest messages when the window is full): when it fills, chat frameworks silently drop the oldest messages.
  3. [Procedural] Loss of instructional integrity (the AI losing its rules because they were pushed out of the window): the rules can be pushed out, for example by a long pasted document, and the AI forgets its role.
  4. [Working] Worse answers near capacity (a very full window makes the AI miss details in the middle): in a very full window, details in the middle get missed.
  5. [Working] Cost and latency (how much each call costs and how long it takes): a full window is slower and costs more.

How NurseOps Now solves it (the three standard solutions first, then mine):

  • [Working] Token counting middleware (code that counts tokens before every AI call and acts when the window gets too full) ⬜ not yet: nothing counts tokens (pieces of words the AI reads and writes; cost is per token) before a call; the history cap (a limit on how many past turns are sent to the AI) below counts turns, not tokens (pieces of words the AI reads and writes; cost is per token) (Plan 1).
  • [Working · Semantic] Dynamic context loading (giving the AI tools to fetch data only when it needs it, instead of putting everything in the prompt) ✅: the AI fetches the case, policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered) or guide with a tool (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) only when it needs them; each result is capped at 12,000 characters, with at most 4 tool (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) rounds (Step 3).
  • [Procedural] System prompt pinning (keeping the rules in a protected place that is sent on every call and never trimmed) ✅: the rules go in the API's separate system field on every call, never in the history, so they can't be trimmed (Step 2).
  • [Working] History cap (a limit on how many past turns are sent to the AI) (ours, a simpler stand-in for a token (the unit the AI reads and is billed by, about three quarters of a word) budget) ✅: only the last 10 turns are sent (Step 2).
  • [Episodic] One thread per user and case (each nurse's conversation about each case is saved separately) (ours) ✅: windows never mix (Step 1).

Implementing Long-Term Memory: External Persistence Architecture (7.3)

What it does. Saves state (everything the app must remember to continue correctly) outside the model so it survives restarts, crashes and new sessions, without keeping personal data longer than needed.

How NurseOps Now solves it.

  • [Episodic] A conversation can be forgotten on request, with an audit row (one line in the log of who did what, when) (Step 9).

Preventing Drift and Hallucinations in Long-Running Agents (7.4)

What it does. Keeps a long-running agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) on its rules (no drift (quality slowly getting worse over time)) and on the facts (no hallucination (the AI stating something that isn't in the sources)) by re-sending instructions and grounding every answer.

The problem:

  1. [Procedural] Context Drift (The Alignment Failure) (the AI slowly forgetting its rules over a long conversation): over many turns the rules fade and the AI starts bending to the user (a strict reviewer slowly becomes an agreeable helper).
  2. [Semantic] Hallucination (The Grounding Failure) (the AI stating made-up facts when the answer isn't in front of it): when the answer isn't in front of it, the AI fills the gap with something plausible and made-up.

How NurseOps Now solves it.

  • [Procedural] Against drift (quality slowly getting worse over time): the rules are sent on every call (Step 2); bad questions and leaky answers are blocked in code (Step 10); and no conversation can produce an approval, because the state machine (a fixed list of allowed status changes) only lets a person approve (Step 7).
  • [Semantic] Against hallucination (the AI stating something that isn't in the sources): answers come only from tools (a function the AI can call, such as policy search), an answer with no source gets a "please check" note, every review quote must match the PDF (a fixed-layout document file) (with one retry), and the output must match a schema (a fixed form the AI's answer must fit) (Step 11).
  • [Procedural] Not yet: an extra reminder of the rules at the end of the prompt (the instructions and text given to the AI), and a 50-turn test that proves the AI doesn't drift (quality slowly getting worse over time) (Plan 5).
