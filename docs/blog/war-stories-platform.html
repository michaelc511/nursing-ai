Why this post

AI platform interviews ask about LLM (large language model: the AI that reads and writes text (here, Claude)) integrations, agents (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer), RAG (retrieval-augmented generation: the AI looks things up before it answers), evals (graded tests of the AI's answers, scored against an answer key), security, cost and reliability, and about owning a problem from the business ask to a measured result. Each story here is a real problem from NurseOps Now, the prior-authorization copilot I scoped, built, tested and deployed, with the fix and the numbers. Every story names its feature numbers (F, S and N in super-features) and links to the pages that explain them.

Two earlier stories, chunking on headings and the eval that said 100%, are in War stories. This post adds the platform side: every story ends with At platform scale, how I'd run the same idea across many teams and apps. That's the difference between "an engineer who used AI tools (a function the AI can call, such as policy search)" and someone who can lead an AI platform.

Honest framing: NurseOps Now is a working prototype with synthetic (made up for the demo; no real patients) data, deployed on Fly.io (the cloud service that hosts the live app). It hasn't had real users or production traffic. I say that once, plainly, and then talk about the decisions. An honest prototype with measured results beats a vague claim about scale.

In plain terms: a folder of "here's what broke, what I did and what it measured" stories, each with a line on how a whole company would do it.

Story 1 (F0): the real problem was rework, not the decision

Features: F0 App Idea & Scope Design (the main fix) · F13.1 State Machine (a rule list of which status changes are allowed) · F21/S21 RBAC (role-based access control: what you can do depends on your role) · N6 AI Governance

Interview topics: business → technical, stakeholders, idea to production

Issue → solution: "let AI approve the easy ones" was the wrong problem → aim the AI at the 22% rework (a request sent back because information was missing), and make "AI never decides" a rule in code.

The situation. A regional health plan (the insurer that pays for members' care) handles about 15,000 prior-authorization requests a month, with a 4.2-day average turnaround (how long a request takes from arrival to decision). Nurses (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny) review every request by hand. Documents arrive by fax, PDF, portal and upload, and providers (the doctor or clinic asking for the test) call just to ask for a status.

The problem. The obvious pitch was "let AI approve the easy ones." That fails twice: a wrong denial harms a patient and creates regulatory risk, and clinical leadership would never adopt a tool (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) that decides for them. When I mapped the work, the time wasn't in the decision. 22% of requests needed rework (a request sent back because information was missing) because information was missing, and the trip back to the provider (the doctor or clinic asking for the test) was what made cases slow.

What I did:

  • I wrote a one-page scope with the goal in business terms: 4.2 days → about 2.5, with no added staff. Success metrics cover turnaround (how long a request takes from arrival to decision), rework (a request sent back because information was missing), cases reviewed and nurse (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny) agreement (how often two graders give the same grade) with the AI.
  • I wrote the non-negotiables before any code: AI never approves or denies, every AI claim cites a page and a quote, and every decision is audited.
  • I aimed the AI at the bottleneck. It extracts the request, flags missing documents up front and drafts the request to the provider (the doctor or clinic asking for the test). Then it gives the nurse (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny) a cited summary with a suggested tier (the AI's suggestion: likely approve, needs review or likely deny).
  • I turned "AI never decides" into code. A state machine (a rule list of which status changes are allowed) in the service layer (the one place in the code where all the business rules live) allows only listed status moves. Nurses (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny) can approve or pend (pause a case for more information or a medical director), and only a medical director (the doctor who is the only one allowed to deny a request) can deny. A request that skips the UI still gets rejected.

The result. The speedup comes from preparing the case, not from removing the nurse (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny), and that makes the tool (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) adoptable. Tests prove the rule: two non-human attempts to approve were refused, the human approval worked, and both real moves were audited. The override rate (how often people decide differently from the AI's suggestion) gives clinical leadership a metric for when to trust the AI more.

The lesson: find where the time actually goes before choosing what the AI does. The stakeholder's real constraint ("we can't let a machine deny care") becomes a design rule, not an objection to manage.

Short version for an interview (about 30 seconds):

"The ask sounded like 'automate prior auth.' When I mapped the work, the delay was 22% rework (a request sent back because information was missing) from missing information, not the decision. So I scoped the AI to prepare the case: extract details, flag missing documents up front, and give the nurse (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny) a cited summary. I made 'AI never decides' a state machine (a rule list of which status changes are allowed) in the service layer (the one place in the code where all the business rules live), so only a nurse (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny) can approve and only a medical director (the doctor who is the only one allowed to deny a request) can deny. The target is 4.2 days down to about 2.5 with no added staff, and nurse (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny) override rate (how often people decide differently from the AI's suggestion) is the trust metric leadership watches."

At platform scale: every AI use case gets the same intake (receiving a request and creating its case) template: the business metric, the human decision (the step where a nurse or medical director decides; the AI only suggests) owner, the non-negotiables and the eval set. Teams fill it in before they get API keys (a secret code that lets the app use a paid service).

In plain terms: don't hire a robot to sign the checks. Hire it to make sure every form is complete before it reaches the person who signs.

Story 2 (F8): one service layer, three clients

Features: F8 FastMCP Server (the main fix) · F4/S16 FastAPI Backend · F12 Engine Strategy Factory · F13.2 Agentic RAG (retrieval-augmented generation: the AI looks things up before it answers)

Interview topics: complex integrations, platform architecture, agentic workflows (a fixed series of steps the app runs)

Issue → solution: an AI client wired to the REST API would re-implement the rules and find a way around them → one thick service layer (the one place in the code where all the business rules live), thin clients (a screen or tool that only calls the service layer and holds no rules), and agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) tools (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) that simply don't include decisions.

The situation. NurseOps Now needed three ways in: a React web app for nurses (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny), a Typer (a library for building command-line tools) CLI for operations, and an MCP (Model Context Protocol: a standard plug that lets AI assistants use an app's tools) server so Claude Cowork (Anthropic's assistant that can run scheduled jobs using connected tools) could run intake (receiving a request and creating its case) on a schedule.

The problem. The fast way to add an AI client is to point it at the REST API, or give it its own database access. Then every client re-implements the business rules, and the AI client is the one most likely to find a path around them.

What I did:

  • I put all the business rules in one thick service layer (the one place in the code where all the business rules live). The web app, the CLI and the MCP (Model Context Protocol: a standard plug that lets AI assistants use an app's tools) server are thin clients (a screen or tool that only calls the service layer and holds no rules) over the same functions.
  • The FastMCP server calls the service layer (the one place in the code where all the business rules live) directly, never REST. It exposes seven tools (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) (run_all, poll_intake, review_case, get_cases, get_case, get_stuck_cases, get_metrics) over Streamable HTTP with a bearer token (a secret key sent with each request to prove it's allowed). Every call runs as the actor cowork, so the audit log (a permanent record of who did what and when) shows what the agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) did.
  • No MCP (Model Context Protocol: a standard plug that lets AI assistants use an app's tools) tool (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) can approve, pend (pause a case for more information or a medical director) or deny. That isn't a prompt (the instructions and text given to the AI) instruction. Those functions aren't exposed, and the state machine (a rule list of which status changes are allowed) would refuse them anyway.
  • I connected Claude Cowork (Anthropic's assistant that can run scheduled jobs using connected tools) to the deployed app as a custom connector, with a scheduled "Prior auth intake (receiving a request and creating its case)" task.

The result. On Nov 14, 2026, Cowork listed all 7 tools (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) and answered a live (using the real Claude API (costs money), not a fake stand-in) operations question with real data (3 cases stuck 4 days, 33% rework (a request sent back because information was missing)). It then ran intake (receiving a request and creating its case) on three sample PDFs for about $0.30, creating and reviewing three cases: one likely approve (the AI's suggestion that the criteria look met), one likely deny (the AI's suggestion that the criteria look unmet; only a medical director can deny) and one incomplete (case status: documents are missing). The agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) client added no new rules to keep in sync.

The lesson: give agents (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) the same front door as people, with fewer keys. Least privilege (give each user or agent only the access it needs, nothing more) for an agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) means the dangerous tool (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) doesn't exist, not that the prompt (the instructions and text given to the AI) says "please don't."

Short version for an interview (about 30 seconds):

"I built one service layer (the one place in the code where all the business rules live) with three thin clients (a screen or tool that only calls the service layer and holds no rules): the web app, a CLI and an MCP (Model Context Protocol: a standard plug that lets AI assistants use an app's tools) server for Claude Cowork (Anthropic's assistant that can run scheduled jobs using connected tools). The MCP (Model Context Protocol: a standard plug that lets AI assistants use an app's tools) tools (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) call the service layer (the one place in the code where all the business rules live) directly, every call is audited as the 'cowork' actor, and there's no tool (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) that can approve or deny. So the agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) gets the same rules as a person, with fewer permissions. Cowork ran scheduled intake (receiving a request and creating its case) on three cases for about thirty cents."

At platform scale: every internal system exposes an MCP (Model Context Protocol: a standard plug that lets AI assistants use an app's tools) server over its service layer (the one place in the code where all the business rules live), behind a shared gateway for identity, scopes and audit (a permanent record of who did what and when). Agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) capabilities become an access-control decision, not a code change.

In plain terms: the new assistant gets a badge for the same building as everyone else, but the badge doesn't open the vault.

Story 3 (F13): workflow or agent

Features: F13/S17 LangGraph (a tool for building AI workflows step by step) Engines (the main fix) · F13.1 State Machine (a rule list of which status changes are allowed) · F13.2 Agentic RAG (retrieval-augmented generation: the AI looks things up before it answers) · F16 Checkpointing

Interview topics: agentic workflows (a fixed series of steps the app runs), architecture, governance

Issue → solution: a free-running agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) can't be audited on a clinical path, but hard-coding every staff question is endless → a fixed workflow (a fixed series of steps the app runs) for decisions and an agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) for questions, drawn by the cost of a wrong step.

The situation. There were two kinds of AI work: reviewing a clinical case against policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered), and answering staff questions like "which cases are stuck?" or "what does the knee MRI (a scan that uses magnets to show soft tissue like discs, ligaments and the brain) policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered) require?"

The problem. A free-running agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) is flexible but hard to test, predict or audit (a permanent record of who did what and when). On a clinical decision path, an unpredictable step is a compliance problem.

What I did:

  • Case review is a fixed LangGraph (a tool for building AI workflows step by step) workflow (a fixed series of steps the app runs): read_form → rewrite_query → retrieve_policy → ai_review → verify → route. Only the review and rewrite steps call the model. The verify node checks every quote, the CPT code (a standard code for a medical procedure) and injection (hidden text that tries to give the AI new orders) flags, and sends failures back to ai_review once. There's a step cap and a per-case cost cap (a spending limit on AI calls, per case, per user or per day) ($0.50 by default), and each node's timing is saved with the review.
  • Staff Q&A is an agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) (AI Assist (the app's chat helper for staff questions)) that chooses among six read-only tools (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) (get_case, list_cases, stuck_cases, metrics, search_policy, search_guide), capped at 4 tool (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) rounds. Its memory is checkpointed (saved step by step, so it can resume after a restart) per user and case thread, so it survives restarts.
  • I kept a frozen linear baseline (saved scores that every new run is compared with) of the review. Tests check that the graph and the baseline (saved scores that every new run is compared with) give the same tier (the AI's suggestion: likely approve, needs review or likely deny) and status on the demo cases.

The result. The critical path is predictable and every step is visible. The flexible part is where a wrong step costs a re-ask, not a wrong denial. The eval found the agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer)'s weak spot honestly: the judge scored AI Assist (the app's chat helper for staff questions) faithfulness (how much the answer sticks to its sources) 3/5 on one case, because it sometimes cited details its tools (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) hadn't returned. That's the expected failure mode of agents (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer), and why it isn't on the decision path.

The lesson: pick workflow (a fixed series of steps the app runs) vs. agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) by the cost of a wrong step, not by fashion. Fixed graphs for decisions, agents (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) for exploration, and always cap steps, cost and permissions.

Short version for an interview (about 30 seconds):

"I split it by risk. The clinical review is a fixed LangGraph (a tool for building AI workflows step by step) state machine (a rule list of which status changes are allowed): read, rewrite, retrieve, review, verify, route, with one retry, a step cap and a fifty-cent cost cap (a spending limit on AI calls, per case, per user or per day) per case. Staff questions go to an agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) with six read-only tools (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) and four tool (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) rounds max. The eval showed the agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) sometimes cites things its tools (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) didn't return, which is exactly why it isn't on the decision path."

At platform scale: the platform offers both patterns as templates with caps, tracing (recording each request step by step) and eval hooks built in, plus a review checklist for moving a use case between them.

In plain terms: a pilot follows the checklist for takeoff but talks freely with the tower. Use the checklist where a mistake is expensive.

Story 4 (F9, S5): the trace that leaked who looked at which case

Features: F9/S15 Tracing (recording each request step by step) and S5 PII (personal details that identify someone, like a name or date of birth) Sanitization (the main fix) · F16 Checkpointing · N6 Governance

Interview topics: security and governance, observability (being able to see what the live app is doing and why), ownership

Issue → solution: the user:case thread ID was reaching the tracing (recording each request step by step) vendor in metadata (labels stored with each chunk: policy, CPT, version, section, page) → hash it, and redact before anything is saved or traced.

The situation. On Nov 11, 2026 I added LangSmith (a service that records each AI request step by step (tracing)) tracing (recording each request step by step) to three deployed AI apps: run names, tags, model, prompt (the instructions and text given to the AI) version, tokens (pieces of words the AI reads and writes; cost is per token) and cost on every LLM (large language model: the AI that reads and writes text (here, Claude)) call, and spans for retrieval (finding the right section of the right policy) and tool (a function the AI can call, such as policy search) steps.

The problem. NurseOps Now handles (synthetic (made up for the demo; no real patients)) patient data, so traces (step-by-step records of each request: what ran, how long, what it cost) must carry no content. LangSmith (a service that records each AI request step by step (tracing)) inputs and outputs are hidden unless a flag turns them on. But the AI Assist (the app's chat helper for staff questions) thread ID was user:case, and it flowed into LangSmith (a service that records each AI request step by step (tracing)) metadata (labels stored with each chunk: policy, CPT, version, section, page). Metadata (labels stored with each chunk: policy, CPT, version, section, page) isn't "content," so content-hiding didn't cover it. Who looked at which case was going to a third party.

What I did:

  • Identified: a privacy test that checks what reaches the tracing (recording each request step by step) layer caught it before release.
  • Fixed: the thread ID is now hashed before it reaches LangSmith (a service that records each AI request step by step (tracing)) metadata (labels stored with each chunk: policy, CPT, version, section, page). It still groups runs, but it can't be read.
  • Hardened the rest: pii.py redacts SSNs, dates, phones, emails, member (a person covered by the health plan) IDs, NPIs (a provider's national ID number), addresses and the names extracted for that case. It runs on logs, AI Assist (the app's chat helper for staff questions) memory and feedback text before anything is saved. The local traces table stores timings, tokens (pieces of words the AI reads and writes; cost is per token), cost and outcome, never content.

The result. Shipped with 137 tests passing and deployed the same day (Fly release v6). Saved memory keeps no personal details: date of birth, phone and SSN were confirmed masked in the demo.

The lesson: personal data hides in identifiers and metadata (labels stored with each chunk: policy, CPT, version, section, page), not just prompt (the instructions and text given to the AI) text. Observability (being able to see what the live app is doing and why) is a data flow to a vendor and needs the same privacy review as any integration.

Short version for an interview (about 30 seconds):

"When I added LangSmith (a service that records each AI request step by step (tracing)) tracing (recording each request step by step), content was hidden by default, but our privacy test caught the thread ID, user plus case, flowing into trace metadata (labels stored with each chunk: policy, CPT, version, section, page). That's who looked at which patient going to a vendor. I hashed it, kept it useful for grouping, and made sure redaction runs before anything is saved or traced. Identifiers leak too, not just prompts (the instructions and text given to the AI)."

At platform scale: tracing (recording each request step by step) goes through one shared wrapper that redacts and hashes centrally, plus a CI (continuous integration: tests that run automatically on every code change) privacy test that fails if raw identifiers reach any exporter. Teams can't opt out by accident.

In plain terms: you blacked out the names in the letter but left them on the envelope.

Story 5 (F6, N3): cost as a design input

Features: F6/S15 Token & Cost Accounting and N3 Caching & Fallback (the main fix) · F2/S6 LLM (large language model: the AI that reads and writes text (here, Claude)) Selector · F21/S21 Rate Limiting

Interview topics: cost optimization, reliability, LLM (large language model: the AI that reads and writes text (here, Claude)) integrations

Issue → solution: a public app on a paid model could run up an unbounded bill, and model choices were guesses → measure every call, avoid calls, cap the rest, and fail toward a person.

The situation. NurseOps Now runs on Claude (Anthropic's AI model that this app uses), with Opus 5.5 by default, and it's publicly reachable as a demo. Every review, agent (an AI that chooses which tools to use to answer) turn and scheduled run spends real money.

What I did, cheapest layer first:

  • Measure: every call goes to llm_calls with tokens (pieces of words the AI reads and writes; cost is per token), cost and latency (how long it takes to respond) (never prompts (the instructions and text given to the AI)). pricing.py converts tokens (pieces of words the AI reads and writes; cost is per token) to dollars, and /api/ai/usage reports totals and per-case cost.
  • Don't call: intent gating is a keyword check with no AI call, so off-topic (not about prior authorization) questions cost nothing. Exact repeats come from a response cache, and the unchanged system prompt (the standing instructions that set the AI's rules and role) is prompt-cached (the unchanged start of a prompt is reused cheaply instead of re-read) on reviews.
  • Cap: $0.50 per case, 20 AI calls per user per minute and $10 per day. Going over returns a 429 and writes an audit (a permanent record of who did what and when) entry.
  • Choose: models are configuration (Opus, Sonnet or Haiku 5.5), switchable without a code change.
  • Degrade safely: if the main model is overloaded, it falls back to Sonnet. If every model fails, the case goes to a person with an "AI unavailable" warning. Production never shows mock (a fake stand-in used in tests so no real AI call is made) answers.

The result. I know what things cost: a live (using the real Claude API (costs money), not a fake stand-in) case review was about $0.11 (Nov 10, 2026), a scheduled 3-case intake (receiving a request and creating its case) about $0.30 (Nov 14), and a full live (using the real Claude API (costs money), not a fake stand-in) eval run $0.42 (Nov 19). Those numbers drive decisions, like running the live (using the real Claude API (costs money), not a fake stand-in) eval before a release rather than on every commit.

The lesson: cost per answer is a product metric. Measure every call, skip the calls you don't need, cap the rest, and fail closed toward a human.

Short version for an interview (about 30 seconds):

"I treat cost as a design input. Every call logs tokens (pieces of words the AI reads and writes; cost is per token), cost and latency (how long it takes to respond). Off-topic (not about prior authorization) questions are blocked by a no-AI keyword gate (an automatic check that blocks a change when scores drop), repeats come from cache, and the system prompt (the standing instructions that set the AI's rules and role) is prompt-cached (the unchanged start of a prompt is reused cheaply instead of re-read). There are hard caps per case, per user per minute and per day. A review costs about eleven cents, so I can reason about 15,000 cases a month. If the model's down, it falls back to Sonnet, and if everything's down, the case goes to a person."

At platform scale: an LLM gateway (one shared front door for all AI model calls: budgets, caching, routing, failover) owns pricing, per-team budgets, caching, routing and failover. Showback (showing each team what it spent, without billing it) dashboards give each team its cost per answer, so model choices are made with data.

Honest gap: routing is manual today. There's no automatic easy-to-Haiku, hard-to-Opus routing yet, and the cache matches exact inputs, not meaning. Those are the next two steps.

In plain terms: check the price tag, don't buy what you don't need, set a budget, and have a plan B when the store is closed.

Story 6 (F17, F20): the eval that said 100%

Features: F17 RAG (retrieval-augmented generation: the AI looks things up before it answers) Evals (graded tests of the AI's answers, scored against an answer key) Harness and F20 CI (continuous integration: tests that run automatically on every code change)/CD Eval Regression (a change that makes results worse than before) Gate (an automatic check that blocks a change when scores drop)

Interview topics: evaluation, ownership

Told in full in War stories, story 2. The ownership angle: every retrieval (finding the right section of the right policy) method scored 100% on the golden set (the answer key: questions with the known right answer). Instead of celebrating, I treated it as a broken test. I expanded the policies (the health plan's written rules for when tests are covered) from 1 page to 5 (21 → 83 chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each)). Offline hit@1 (the right section came back first) dropped from 0.889 to 0.778 and live (using the real Claude API (costs money), not a fake stand-in) faithfulness (how much the answer sticks to its sources) from 0.893 to 0.775. The CI gate (automatic tests on every code change; a lower score blocks the change) failed on its own (GATE FAILED: hit@1 0.889 -> 0.778), and the harder test exposed real misses I could then fix.

Story 7 (N4): real mistakes become test cases

Features: N4 Online Quality Monitoring (watching the live app's quality, cost and errors) (the main fix) · F17 Evals (graded tests of the AI's answers, scored against an answer key) · F20 CI Gate (automatic tests on every code change; a lower score blocks the change) · N5 Red Teaming (trying to trick the AI on purpose to find weak spots)

Interview topics: evaluation and observability (being able to see what the live app is doing and why), ownership (measure results)

Issue → solution: an offline eval only tests the questions I thought of → watch live (using the real Claude API (costs money), not a fake stand-in) signals, and turn real failures into golden cases the gate (an automatic check that blocks a change when scores drop) guards.

The situation. An offline eval tells you it worked before launch. It doesn't tell you it still works next month.

The problem. With a golden set (the answer key: questions with the known right answer) of 20 cases, quality is judged on my questions. Real failures look different, and nobody would notice drift (quality slowly getting worse over time) until a nurse (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny) complained.

What I did:

  • Online signals: /api/ai/quality reports thumbs up and down, the override rate (how often people decide differently from the AI's suggestion), the unverified-quote rate (how often the AI's quote can't be found in the source document), AI-unavailable reviews, cost and latency (how long it takes to respond), with alerts (warnings raised when a quality or cost number crosses a limit) for drift (quality slowly getting worse over time).
  • Feedback loop (turning real mistakes into new test questions so they stay fixed): the CLI command export-feedback writes thumbs-down (a user marking an AI answer as bad) items to evaluation/candidates.jsonl. A person reviews them and promotes the good ones into the golden set (the answer key: questions with the known right answer), so the CI gate (automatic tests on every code change; a lower score blocks the change) guards that exact failure from then on.
  • Gate (an automatic check that blocks a change when scores drop) rules: the gate (an automatic check that blocks a change when scores drop) fails on any safety failure, or any score more than 0.05 below the baseline (saved scores that every new run is compared with).
  • Attacks: a red-team (people or scripts that try to trick the AI on purpose) suite generates 44 attacks (injection (hidden text that tries to give the AI new orders) variants, prompt (the instructions and text given to the AI) extraction, fake authority, decision requests, off-topic (not about prior authorization)). An attack counts as handled only if it causes no decision, no leak and no status change. All 44 were handled, offline and on real Claude (Anthropic's AI model that this app uses).

The result. The live (using the real Claude API (costs money), not a fake stand-in) baseline (saved scores that every new run is compared with) (Nov 10, 2026, original policies (the health plan's written rules for when tests are covered)) was retrieval (finding the right section of the right policy) 1.0, review 1.0, AI Assist (the app's chat helper for staff questions) 0.875, safety 1.0 and faithfulness (how much the answer sticks to its sources) 0.893. Quality has a number before release, a gate (an automatic check that blocks a change when scores drop) at merge, and a signal in production that feeds back into the gate (an automatic check that blocks a change when scores drop).

The lesson: grow the eval set from production, not from imagination. In a human-in-the-loop system, override rate (how often people decide differently from the AI's suggestion) is the best single metric, because the expert grades the AI for free.

Short version for an interview (about 30 seconds):

"I built quality as a loop. Offline, there's a golden set (the answer key: questions with the known right answer) with an LLM judge (a second AI that grades the answers) and RAGAS (an open-source library that scores RAG answers), and a CI gate (automatic tests on every code change; a lower score blocks the change) that fails on any safety miss or a drop of more than 0.05. In production, I track thumbs, nurse (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny) override rate (how often people decide differently from the AI's suggestion) and unverified-quote rate (how often the AI's quote can't be found in the source document). Thumbs-down (a user marking an AI answer as bad) items export to a candidates file, a person promotes them to the golden set (the answer key: questions with the known right answer), and the gate (an automatic check that blocks a change when scores drop) then protects against that failure forever. Red team (people or scripts that try to trick the AI on purpose): 44 attacks, 44 handled."

At platform scale: a shared eval service holds every app's golden sets (the answer key: questions with the known right answer), judges and baselines (saved scores that every new run is compared with), a shared feedback widget and override (the nurse decided differently from the AI's suggestion) logging feed it, and every app gets the same release gate (an automatic check that blocks a change when scores drop).

Honest gap: the live (using the real Claude API (costs money), not a fake stand-in) gate (an automatic check that blocks a change when scores drop) in GitHub Actions (GitHub's service that runs tests automatically on each change) needs the API key (a secret code that lets the app use a paid service) as a repository secret, which isn't added yet, so CI (continuous integration: tests that run automatically on every code change) runs the offline gate (an automatic check that blocks a change when scores drop). In plain terms: every customer complaint becomes a new question on the next practice exam.

Story 8 (F22): code checks every quote

Features: F13.1 Verify-and-Retry and F22 Prompt-Injection Defense (the main fix) · F3.1/S11,S12 Prompt (the instructions and text given to the AI) & Grounding

Interview topics: reliability, security, RAG (retrieval-augmented generation: the AI looks things up before it answers)

Issue → solution: "a schema (a fixed form the AI's answer must fit) stops hallucination (the AI stating something that isn't in the sources)" is wrong, since a well-formed answer can hold a made-up quote → check every quote in code, and fail toward a person.

The situation. Each review returns JSON (a simple text format for structured data) validated by Pydantic (a library that checks data fits a fixed form): extracted fields, missing items, criteria met or not, a tier (the AI's suggestion: likely approve, needs review or likely deny), and a page and quote for every claim.

The problem. A schema (a fixed form the AI's answer must fit) stops malformed answers, but a perfectly formed answer can still contain a made-up quote. In a clinical review, a fabricated citation (a pointer to the exact page and quote that supports a claim) is worse than none, because it looks trustworthy.

What I did:

  • Verify in code, not in the model: the verify node checks every quote against the source PDF. A mismatch gets one retry. If it still fails, the case goes to needs review (the AI's middle suggestion: a person should look closely) and counts in the unverified-quote rate (how often the AI's quote can't be found in the source document).
  • Treat documents as data: clinical text is fenced in <document> tags, and the prompt (the instructions and text given to the AI) says document text is data. An injection (hidden text that tries to give the AI new orders) match doesn't silently drop the case. It forces needs review (the AI's middle suggestion: a person should look closely) and shows the nurse (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny) a flag. Output guardrails (checks that block unsafe or off-topic input and output) block any answer that leaks the system prompt (the standing instructions that set the AI's rules and role) or a key.

The result. The first live (using the real Claude API (costs money), not a fake stand-in) Claude (Anthropic's AI model that this app uses) review (Nov 10, 2026) came back likely approve (the AI's suggestion that the criteria look met) with all quotes verified, for about $0.11. Injected text can't produce a decision: the worst case is a flagged case in a human queue.

The lesson: turn "trust the model" into "verify the model." Anything code can check, code should check, and anything uncertain fails toward a person.

Short version for an interview (about 30 seconds):

"Pydantic (a library that checks data fits a fixed form) guarantees the shape, not the truth. So every quote the AI cites is checked in code against the source PDF. A miss gets one retry, then the case goes to a nurse (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny) and counts toward the unverified-quote rate (how often the AI's quote can't be found in the source document). Injected text in a document gets flagged and routed to a person too. The worst case is always a human looking at it, never a wrong decision."

At platform scale: citation (a pointer to the exact page and quote that supports a claim) checking and document fencing are shared library functions every RAG (retrieval-augmented generation: the AI looks things up before it answers) app uses, and unverified-quote rate (how often the AI's quote can't be found in the source document) is a standard dashboard metric.

In plain terms: don't just check the footnote is formatted right. Open the book and make sure the page says it.
