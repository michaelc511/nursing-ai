Why this post

NurseOps Now reads prior authorization (a health plan approving a test or treatment before it happens) requests, finds the matching rule in a coverage policy (the health plan's rules for when a test is covered) and drafts a cited summary for a nurse (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny). A person always makes the decision. But a nurse (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny) can only trust the draft if the AI finds the right rule and quotes it correctly, every time, and keeps doing so after every change we make.

"It looked right when I tried it" is not proof. This post is the plan to prove, with numbers, that the answers are good at every step of the system: which questions we test with, what each check measures, what we have done so far, and what we will do next.

In plain terms: we are writing an exam with an answer key (the known right answers the AI is graded against), giving it to the AI after every change, and refusing to ship any change that lowers the grade. Then we check that the exam is hard enough, that the grader is fair, and that real mistakes in production become new exam questions.

Step 1: Ingestion

What it does. Turns each policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered) PDF into small, searchable pieces (chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each)), one per rule, before any question arrives.

What we built. backend/app/rag/chunker.py splits the PDF by clause (one rule in a policy, such as §2.A) (§2.A, §2.B …), not by a fixed number of characters (see below). Each chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) starts with a header that names the policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered), version, section and page, so it still makes sense when read alone. Each chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) is then turned into a vector (text turned into a list of numbers so similar meanings sit close together) (sentence-transformers (a library that turns text into vectors)) and stored in Chroma (the database that stores the policy vectors). In Step 0 the knee policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered) grew from 8 to 29 chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each).

Example (real chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each)).

[RHP-IMG-022 v2.2 | MRI knee | CPT 73721 | §2.A Covered when ONE of the following is documented | page 1]
Suspected meniscus or ligament tear with mechanical symptoms (locking, catching,
giving way) or a positive exam test (McMurray, Lachman, drawer), AND a knee X-ray,
AND failure of at least 4–6 weeks of conservative care (PT, activity change, NSAIDs).

[RHP-IMG-022 v2.2 | MRI knee | CPT 73721 | §6.B Definitions | page 3]
Mechanical symptoms: locking, catching or the knee giving way during normal activity.

By clause (one rule in a policy, such as §2.A), not by characters. Many RAG (retrieval-augmented generation: the AI looks things up before it answers) apps cut text every N characters (for example every 500). That can split a rule in half: one chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) says "6 weeks of PT (physical therapy: guided exercise treatment)" and the next says "AND a knee X-ray (a quick image that shows bones)", so the search may return half a rule. Here the chunker (the code that cuts documents into chunks) cuts where the policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered) itself does: at each numbered section ("2.") and each lettered rule ("A."). Characters are only a fallback: if one clause (one rule in a policy, such as §2.A) is longer than 1,200 characters, it is split again by paragraph, then line, then sentence, with 150 characters of overlap (characters repeated between neighboring chunks so nothing is lost at a cut) (MAX_CHARS and OVERLAP in chunker.py). No clause (one rule in a policy, such as §2.A) in the current policies (the health plan's written rules for when tests are covered) is that long, so every chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) is one whole rule.

Part C will measure it, comparing fixed-size (cutting text every N characters, wherever that lands) chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) with clause (one rule in a policy, such as §2.A) chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) on the same questions.

How it's checked. Unit tests (small automatic tests of one piece of code) confirm every chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) has its CPT (a standard code for a medical procedure), version, label and page (tests/test_rag.py).

In plain terms: cutting a binder into index (the searchable store of chunks and their vectors) cards, one rule per card, with the binder's name and page written on top of each card.

Step 2: Read the request

What it does. Reads the incoming request before any AI runs: gets the text of every page (with OCR (reading the text in a picture of a page, like a fax) when a fax has no text layer), finds the CPT code (a standard code for a medical procedure) to pick the right policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered), and scans every page for hidden instructions.

What we built. The read_form step of the review workflow (a fixed series of steps the app runs) (backend/app/services/review_graph.py), using backend/app/services/parsing.py. OCR (reading the text in a picture of a page, like a fax) runs on this machine (RapidOCR (an OCR library that runs on this computer, so nothing leaves it)), so no patient data leaves it. The CPT code (a standard code for a medical procedure) is read by code, not by the AI, so the wrong policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered) can't be picked by a guess. Claude (Anthropic's AI model that this app uses) also reads the PDF itself in step 7, but this text is what code uses to verify quotes in step 8.

Step 5: Hybrid search

What it does. Finds the best chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) in two ways at once: by meaning (vectors (text turned into lists of numbers so similar meanings sit close together)) and by exact words (BM25 (a classic keyword-search score) keyword search (search for the exact words in the question)). The two lists are merged with Reciprocal Rank Fusion (a simple way to merge two ranked lists into one).

What we built. backend/app/rag/index.py. The search first filters to the policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered) for the request's CPT code (a standard code for a medical procedure), then searches by meaning and by keyword. Either method can be switched off (HYBRID_SEARCH), which Part C uses.

What we built. A cross-encoder (a model that reads the question and a text together to score the match) (ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2) in backend/app/rag/index.py, which can be switched off with RERANK.

Step 8: Output checks

What it does. Code checks every quote in the AI's review against the actual text of the request and the policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered) PDFs. A quote that can't be found sends the case to a person with a warning.

What we built. Quote verification (code checking that every quote really appears in the source PDF) in the review workflow (a fixed series of steps the app runs), with one retry. The output is also checked for leaks such as the system prompt (the standing instructions that set the AI's rules and role) or keys.

Example. In the latest run, all of case 2's quotes were found in the source documents (quotes=100%). If Claude (Anthropic's AI model that this app uses) had written "PT (physical therapy: guided exercise treatment) completed for 6 weeks", which isn't in the note, the check would flag "Quote not found" and the case would go to needs review (the AI's middle suggestion: a person should look closely).

How it's checked. The quote-verified rate (share of the AI's quotes found word for word in the sources) in the golden set (the answer key: questions with the known right answer) (it must be at least 80%) and as a live (using the real Claude API (costs money), not a fake stand-in) alert (more than 20% unverified triggers a warning).

In plain terms: a fact-checker who opens every cited page to confirm the quote is really there.

Step 9: Human decision and audit

What it does. The AI's work ends at a suggestion. A person makes the decision, and every step is written to an audit log (a permanent record of who did what and when).

What we built. backend/app/services/decision.py. The rules are enforced in code, not just hidden in the screen:

  • Only a nurse (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny) or a medical director (the doctor who is the only one allowed to deny a request) can decide, and a written rationale (the written reason for a decision) is required.
  • A nurse (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny) can approve or pend (pause a case for more information or a medical director). Only a medical director (the doctor who is the only one allowed to deny a request) can deny.
  • If the person disagrees with the AI's tier (the AI's suggestion: likely approve, needs review or likely deny) (approves a "likely deny (the AI's suggestion that the criteria look unmet; only a medical director can deny)", or denies a "likely approve (the AI's suggestion that the criteria look met)"), it is saved as an override (the nurse decided differently from the AI's suggestion).
  • Each decision writes an audit (a permanent record of who did what and when) row: who, role, decision, rationale (the written reason for a decision), the AI tier (the AI's suggestion: likely approve, needs review or likely deny), the override (the nurse decided differently from the AI's suggestion) flag and the time.

Example (case 2). The case is Incomplete (case status: documents are missing), so the drafted request goes to Dr. Okafor: "…please send the physical therapy (guided exercise treatment) records showing visit dates, number of weeks and the patient's response. Policy (the health plan's written rules for when a test is covered) RHP-IMG-022 requires at least 4–6 weeks of conservative care (non-surgical treatment such as physical therapy or medicine)." If a nurse (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny) tried to deny it, the app would refuse with the real message: "Only a Medical Director (the doctor who is the only one allowed to deny a request) can deny (adverse determination (a denial; only a medical director may issue one)). Pend (pause a case for more information or a medical director) it for MD review."

How it's checked. Unit tests (small automatic tests of one piece of code) for the role rules and status changes. The override rate (how often people decide differently from the AI's suggestion) is a live (using the real Claude API (costs money), not a fake stand-in) quality signal in step 12: nurses (the reviewer who checks requests; can approve or pend, not deny) disagreeing with the AI more than 30% of the time raises an alert.

In plain terms: the AI prepares the file, but only a person can sign it, and a camera records who signed what and when.
