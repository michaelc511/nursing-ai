War story 1 (S14): half the configuration steps, fixed by chunking on headings

Features: S14 Clause-Aware Chunking (cutting a policy at each clause so every piece is one whole rule) (the main fix) · S4 Metadata (labels stored with each chunk: policy, CPT, version, section, page) Extraction · F18/S9 Hybrid Search (meaning search and keyword search combined) · F10/S10 Cross-Encoder (a model that reads the question and a text together to score the match) Re-ranking (a second model reorders the top results) · N1 Document Parsing · N2 Incremental Re-index (rebuild the searchable store after documents change) & Doc Versioning

Issue → solution: answers came back with half a procedure or the wrong product version → chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) by the document's own headings, and label every chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule).

War story: "The assistant kept giving half the configuration steps"

The situation. At work we built an internal assistant for support engineers and implementation consultants. It answered questions from our enterprise software documentation: admin guides, configuration manuals, release notes, integration specs and internal domain knowledge like billing rules, approval workflows (a fixed series of steps the app runs) and tax handling by region. The documents were long and heavily structured, with nested headings, numbered steps, prerequisites, notes and "applies to version X" callouts.

The problem. Answers were often almost right. Someone would ask "how do I set up multi-level approval for purchase orders?" and get steps 1–4 but not steps 5–7. Or the answer missed the warning that this only works when a certain module is enabled. Sometimes it mixed instructions from two product versions, or pulled a similar-looking procedure from a different module. For a consultant configuring a customer's system, half the steps or the wrong version is worse than no answer.

What we tried:

  1. Fixed-size (cutting text every N characters, wherever that lands) chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) (N tokens (pieces of words the AI reads and writes; cost is per token)). These were the easiest to build and the worst results. The cutoffs landed in the middle of procedures, so the prerequisites ended up in one chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) and the steps in another. Retrieval (finding the right section of the right policy) found one piece and the model answered as if it had the whole thing.
  2. Fixed-size (cutting text every N characters, wherever that lands) with overlap (characters repeated between neighboring chunks so nothing is lost at a cut). This helped a little, but it bloated the index (the searchable store of chunks and their vectors), still cut long procedures, and filled the top results with near-duplicates.
  3. Paragraph or sentence splitting. The pieces were cleaner, but they had no context. A chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) saying "Set the threshold (the cut-off a score must stay above) to the approval limit" didn't say which module, feature or version it belonged to, so purchase-order and expense-approval chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) looked the same to the embeddings (text turned into numbers so similar meanings sit close together).
  4. Embedding-based semantic chunking (cutting text where the meaning changes). It was smarter in theory, but the boundaries shifted every time we re-indexed. It also didn't match how the docs were organized, so citations (pointers to the exact page and quote that support each claim) were hard to trace back.
  5. Stuffing whole documents into the prompt (the instructions and text given to the AI). That worked for a short release note but was far too slow and expensive for a 300-page admin guide.

What actually worked: chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) by the document's own headings, and label every chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule).

  • We split on structure, not size. One chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) per heading section or per complete procedure, following the doc's own hierarchy (H1 > H2 > H3). A procedure is never cut in half, and its prerequisites and warnings stay with the steps.
  • We added a contextual header (a line on each chunk naming its policy, version, section and page) to every chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule). Each one starts with a breadcrumb such as [Admin Guide v<version> | Procurement module | Approvals > Multi-level approval | page/URL]. The embedding (text turned into numbers so similar meanings sit close together) now carries where the chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) lives (using the real Claude API (costs money), not a fake stand-in), and every citation (a pointer to the exact page and quote that supports a claim) points to an exact section.
  • We set a size cap as a fallback. Only sections over a set length are split further, and the split happens at sub-headings or step boundaries, never mid-step. Boilerplate like nav menus, page headers, footers and legal text is stripped first.
  • We put metadata (labels stored with each chunk: policy, CPT, version, section, page) on every chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule): product, module, version, doc type, section and audience (admin or end user). Search pre-filters on these before ranking, so a question about version 2024.2 of Procurement never gets an answer from 2022 or from the Expenses module.
  • We added hybrid search (meaning search and keyword search combined) plus re-ranking (a second model reorders the top results). Keyword search (search for the exact words in the question) (BM25 (a classic keyword-search score)) catches exact terms like field names, error codes and setting names that embeddings (text turned into numbers so similar meanings sit close together) tend to blur. Embeddings (text turned into numbers so similar meanings sit close together) catch the meaning, and a cross-encoder (a model that reads the question and a text together to score the match) re-ranks the results so the most relevant section comes first.
  • We re-index (rebuild the searchable store after documents change) per document. When a new release ships, only that guide's chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) are replaced, and the version lives (using the real Claude API (costs money), not a fake stand-in) in both the header and the metadata (labels stored with each chunk: policy, CPT, version, section, page).

The result. Fewer, complete chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each). Answers came back with every step plus the prerequisites and warnings, citations (pointers to the exact page and quote that support each claim) linked to the exact section, and the version mix-ups stopped. Support engineers began trusting the answers enough to paste the cited section straight into customer tickets.

Trade-offs I'd mention: heading-based chunking needs a parser per format (HTML help sites, PDFs, Word, Confluence exports), and it depends on authors actually using headings properly. For unstructured content like old wiki pages or exported emails, I'd fall back to fixed-size (cutting text every N characters, wherever that lands) chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each) with overlap (characters repeated between neighboring chunks so nothing is lost at a cut) and measure the retrieval (finding the right section of the right policy) hit rate on a test question set rather than assume.

The lesson: chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) the way people use the document. Consultants follow one procedure or one feature section at a time, so each chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) should be one complete section, labeled with product, module and version.

Short version for an interview (about 30 seconds):

"Our support assistant over enterprise product docs kept returning half a configuration procedure or mixing product versions. We tried fixed-size (cutting text every N characters, wherever that lands) chunks (small pieces of a document; here, one policy rule each), overlap (characters repeated between neighboring chunks so nothing is lost at a cut), paragraph splitting and semantic chunking (cutting text where the meaning changes), and they all cut procedures or lost context. What fixed it was chunking by the doc's own headings, one complete section per chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule), with a breadcrumb header on each chunk (a small piece of a document; here, one policy rule) and metadata (labels stored with each chunk: policy, CPT, version, section, page) for product, module and version that we pre-filter on. Add hybrid search (meaning search and keyword search combined) and a re-ranker (a second model that reorders the top results), and answers came back complete and cited to the exact section."

War story 2 (F17, F20): the eval said 100%, fixed by making the test as hard as real life

Features: F17 RAG (retrieval-augmented generation: the AI looks things up before it answers) Evals (graded tests of the AI's answers, scored against an answer key) Harness and F20 CI (continuous integration: tests that run automatically on every code change)/CD Eval Regression (a change that makes results worse than before) Gate (an automatic check that blocks a change when scores drop) (the main fix)

Issue → solution: every retrieval (finding the right section of the right policy) method scored 100%, so the test proved nothing → make the test documents as long and overlapping as the real ones, and let the CI gate (automatic tests on every code change; a lower score blocks the change) judge.

War story: "Our eval said 100%, and that was the problem"

The situation. At work we built an internal assistant that answered support and implementation questions from our enterprise product documentation: admin guides, configuration manuals and release notes. Before rolling it out, we built an eval: a set of real questions with known right answers, a retrieval (finding the right section of the right policy) score for each, and a CI gate (automatic tests on every code change; a lower score blocks the change) that blocked any change that lowered the score.

The problem. Every retrieval (finding the right section of the right policy) method we tried scored 100%. Keyword search (search for the exact words in the question), embeddings (text turned into numbers so similar meanings sit close together) and hybrid search (meaning search and keyword search combined) all found the right section every time. That looked great, but it meant the test was useless. The test documents were short, clean sample pages, and the assistant first narrows the search to one product guide, so it only had to choose among a handful of sections. A test everyone passes proves nothing.

What we did. We made the test documents realistic. We replaced the sample pages with full-length guides, the way customers actually see them: glossaries, prerequisites, troubleshooting, version notes, and release notes that repeat the admin guide's wording. Much of the new text deliberately overlapped the procedures we were testing, the way real documentation does. We kept the original sections unchanged so existing answers and citations (pointers to the exact page and quote that support each claim) still held, bumped the document versions and rebuilt the index (the searchable store of chunks and their vectors).

The result. The scores dropped, and the CI gate (automatic tests on every code change; a lower score blocks the change) caught the drop on its own and failed the build. That was the gate (an automatic check that blocks a change when scores drop) doing its job. Because we had changed the documents on purpose, we reviewed the drop, then saved a new baseline (saved scores that every new run is compared with).

What it exposed. Real problems the easy test had hidden:

  • A question about which permissions a setup needs returned a release-notes mention instead of the prerequisites section.
  • Some questions had two correct sections, so the answer key (the known right answers the AI is graded against) had to allow more than one right answer.
  • Glossary definitions started outranking the actual procedure, the way they compete in real docs.

What's next. Before changing the retrieval (finding the right section of the right policy), we ran the live (using the real Claude API (costs money), not a fake stand-in) eval several times to separate real drops from run-to-run noise (small random differences between runs of the same test).

The lesson: make the test as hard as real life, even when the numbers get worse. Lower numbers from an honest test beat 100% from an easy one.

Short version for an interview (about 30 seconds):

"Our first eval scored 100% on every retrieval (finding the right section of the right policy) method, which told me the test was too easy. We swapped the short sample pages for full-length, overlapping product docs, the scores dropped, and our CI gate (automatic tests on every code change; a lower score blocks the change) caught it on its own. The harder test exposed real misses: release notes beating the prerequisites section, questions with two right answers, and definitions outranking procedures. Lower numbers from an honest test beat 100% from an easy one."

What this proves

The choices in NurseOps Now aren't one-offs for medical policies (the health plan's written rules for when tests are covered). Cutting at the document's own structure (story 1) and testing on documents as hard as the real ones (story 2) solve the same problems in enterprise product documentation, and each story's NurseOps Now table shows the measured proof.

All NurseOps Now policies (the health plan's written rules for when tests are covered) and cases are synthetic (made up for the demo; no real patients). No real patient data is used.
